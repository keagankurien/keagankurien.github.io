Chelsea FC

How I think about
football content.

"Two words. Right photo. Right moment. That's the job."

Chelsea posted "These two. 💙🇧🇷" after beating Spurs last week. 33K likes. Two words. That's not luck. That's someone knowing exactly when to say less.

I engage with Chelsea's channels the way I engage with their matches. As a fan, yes, but also analytically. Each channel has a different job, a different tone, a different measure of what good looks like. That's what the section below is about.

What I've noticed
Strength

X post-match is the strongest output

"Shooooot. 🫣": 101K impressions. "Enzo from range! 🌊": 105K. One emoji, player name, action verb. It's a formula but it doesn't feel like one. That's the standard I'd match.

Idea

Fan data through content, not banners

End-of-season carousel with a WhatsApp CTA. Prediction questions before matchday. The clubs pulling first-party data right now do it through content fans actually want.

How I read the channels

Each platform is a different conversation.

X / Twitter
Live commentary

X is where matchday actually happens. The job is speed. Post within seconds of a goal, a VAR call, a red card. Short lines, one emoji max, player name first. The copy carries the emotion; the clip confirms it. Anything longer than two lines loses the moment.

What good looks like
"Enzo from range! 🌊": 105K impressions
"Shooooot. 🫣": 101K impressions
"These two. 💙🇧🇷": 33K likes
The principle: say less. Fans bring the feeling themselves.
TikTok
Personality-led

TikTok has the most room for personality. It's where Chelsea can be playful, self-aware and fan-facing in a way that would feel off-brand on Instagram. Behind-the-scenes moments, humour, trending audio, looser energy. Retention matters most. The first two seconds decide whether people stay.

What good looks like
Reaction formats. Trending sounds timed to a match moment. Player content that feels unguarded rather than produced.
The principle: personality over polish. The less corporate it feels, the better it performs.
Instagram
Visual & considered

Instagram is where the brand lives visually. The feed is more controlled: better photography, cleaner graphics, captions that support the image rather than carry it. Carousels tend to drive saves, which is where Instagram value often shows up. Stories is where fan interaction happens: polls, Q&As, match reactions.

What good looks like
Player photography fans screenshot. End-of-season carousels with CTAs. Stories polls that capture preference data without feeling like surveys.
The principle: looks matter here in a way they don't on X. One bad graphic in the feed damages the whole aesthetic.
WhatsApp
Direct & practical

WhatsApp is a different job entirely. The audience has opted in. They want information, not entertainment. Matchday logistics, lineup drops, result updates, pre-season news. Calm and direct. No emoji spam, no marketing language. Like a trusted mate sending you what you actually need to know before a game.

What good looks like
Lineup confirmed. Kick-off 3pm. Gates open 1:30pm. Travel advice for Sunderland away.
The principle: utility first. Fans who sign up already love the club. They don't need selling to.
Facebook
Community & reach

Facebook skews older and reaches parts of the global fanbase that aren't on TikTok. Longer-form content and match recaps perform well. Fans comment, share and tag friends in a way that doesn't happen on Instagram. Less about speed, more about completeness.

What good looks like
Full match highlights. Player feature videos. Historical content that sparks nostalgia and comment threads.
The principle: Facebook fans want the full story. Give them something to discuss.

One prompt doesn't cover five platforms. The caption written for X will kill the Instagram post. The TikTok that works at 11pm won't land on Facebook at 9am. Every channel needs its own brief, even when the content is the same clip.

Who I am

Grew up in Dubai.
Fell in love with Chelsea
at eight years old.

I became a Chelsea fan on the night of the 2008 Champions League final. I was eight years old. I watched my uncle and cousin cry when we lost on penalties, and I thought: if it makes grown men cry, there must be something worth caring about.

The first time I went to a match was the 2016/17 season. Chelsea beat City 3-1 at the Etihad. Hazard, Costa, Willian, a Cahill own goal, a red card, a fight in the tunnel. I was sat in the City end because my uncle got the tickets through work, so every time Chelsea scored I had to hold my cousin back from celebrating. I fake-celebrated the Cahill own goal with the City fans around me. Gave Pep a high five as the players were arriving. I felt connected to the club in a way I hadn't before, and that's never really left. During my first year of university in Singapore, I'd stay up through the night for 5am kickoffs, catch a couple of hours sleep, and make my 10am lecture. You do that for something you actually care about.

Marketing clicked at a football league startup in Dubai called Line Up Social Sports, an internship between my third and fourth year at university. I posted match highlights and watched per-game revenue go up. Not a module, not a lecture. Just watching something I made move a number that mattered. That environment moved fast. Feedback was immediate. I've been looking for that feeling in every role since.

I watch football the same way I watch film: interested in the decisions, not just the outcome. Why didn't the winger take on his full-back? Tactical instruction or the player bottling it? I notice atmospheres too. Stamford Bridge against Barcelona is a different animal to the same ground on a mid-table Tuesday. That's the lens I bring to content. It keeps me close to what's actually happening in the game and around it.

I built this portfolio myself, teaching myself the tools as I went. I'm currently reading Chris Sutton's How to Fix Modern Football. His argument that the modern game has lost something the old game had (atmosphere, risk, personality) is the kind of question I find myself thinking about when I'm watching content as much as matches. That's the lens I bring to this.
